What is a diffusion model?

A diffusion model learns to turn pure noise into data by reversing a gradual noising process[1][2]. It has two halves:

  • Forward (fixed, no learning) — repeatedly add a little Gaussian noise to a data point until, after $T$ steps, nothing is left but noise $\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
  • Reverse (learned) — a neural network is trained to undo one noising step at a time, so we can start from noise and walk back to a brand-new sample.

What does "diffusion" mean?

The name comes from physics. The original diffusion model was inspired by nonequilibrium thermodynamics: slowly destroy structure in the data with a forward diffusion process, then learn a reverse process that restores it[1]. Diffusion itself is the everyday process where particles packed into one spot gradually spread out until they fill the whole space evenly — a drop of ink dissolving in water, or a puff of smoke thinning into a room.

Now zoom in on a single particle and photograph it at tiny time intervals. Step to step, it does not move in a straight line — it gets nudged in a random direction each instant. Those nudges are Gaussian: each step is drawn from a normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. So the particle's path is a Gaussian random walk, and the cloud of many such particles is what we see spreading out.

Here is the key idea that makes generation possible: if every step is a known Gaussian, and we could figure out the distribution of each step, then we could run the whole walk backward — start from the fully spread-out noise and retrace the steps until the particles gather back into their original shape. That a diffusion can be run backward in time at all — its time-reversal is itself a diffusion — is a classical result[6]. A diffusion model is exactly that: the spreading is the fixed forward process, and a neural network learns to reverse it. You can watch this happen on real data in the panels below.

    Forward diffusion

    The data $x_0$ — points forming a dinosaur (one of the Datasaurus shapes[5]) — is standardized to zero mean and unit variance. That standardization is what makes the fully-noised end state exactly $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, the distribution we later sample from. Forward diffusion dissolves this shape into noise; the trained model learns to rebuild it.

    Each step adds Gaussian noise scaled by a small variance $\beta_t$[2]:

    $$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big)$$

    Chaining the steps has a closed form, so we can jump straight to any timestep $t$ without simulating the ones before it. With $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$:

    $$x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

    As $t$ grows, $\bar\alpha_t\to 0$: the data term fades and the noise term takes over, so $x_T\approx\mathcal{N}(0,I)$. Here $T=400$ and $\bar\alpha_t$ follows a cosine schedule (Nichol & Dhariwal[3]), which holds onto the signal longer than a linear ramp before fading to pure noise — small steps, so the shape erodes smoothly.

    Training the denoiser

    The reverse step is also approximately Gaussian. Rather than predict its mean directly, the network predicts the noise $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ that was added — a simpler, better-behaved target[2]. This noise-prediction objective is exactly denoising score matching[8]: the predicted noise is, up to scale, the score of the noised data, so training one network across every noise level recovers the score-based model[7]. Training minimizes the mean-squared error between the true and predicted noise:

    $$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\,t,\,\varepsilon}\Big[\,\big\lVert\, \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t\big)\,\big\rVert^2\,\Big]$$

    One training step: draw a data point $x_0$, a random timestep $t\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\dots,T\}$, and noise $\varepsilon$; build the noisy $x_t$ with the closed form above; ask the network for $\varepsilon_\theta$; take a gradient step on the MSE. Since any $t$ is reachable directly, no step-by-step simulation is needed during training.

    Reverse sampling

    To generate, start from noise $x_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and undo one step at a time. Substituting the predicted noise into the reverse posterior gives its mean[2]:

    $$\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big)$$

    and each ancestral step re-injects a little fresh noise — this is what makes sampling stochastic:

    $$x_{t-1} = \mu_\theta(x_t, t) + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,z,\qquad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\ \ (t>0),\qquad x_0=\mu_\theta(x_1, 1)$$

    To keep sampling stable, each step also clamps the implied $x_0$ estimate back into the data range (clip_denoised) — standard practice that stops an imperfect model from diverging to infinity.
