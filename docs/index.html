What is a diffusion model?

A diffusion model learns to turn pure noise into data by reversing a gradual noising process. It has two halves:

  • Forward (fixed, no learning) — repeatedly add a little Gaussian noise to a data point until, after $T$ steps, nothing is left but noise $\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I)$.
  • Reverse (learned) — a neural network is trained to undo one noising step at a time, so we can start from noise and walk back to a brand-new sample.

Everything below uses one toy dataset — points arranged as a dinosaur — so you can watch each equation happen on a 2-D scatter plot. The model was trained offline in PyTorch; the browser only replays the precomputed snapshots.

What does "diffusion" mean?

The name comes from physics. Diffusion (확산) is the everyday process where particles packed into one spot gradually spread out until they fill the whole space evenly — a drop of ink dissolving in water, or a puff of smoke thinning into a room.

Now zoom in on a single particle and photograph it at tiny time intervals. Step to step, it does not move in a straight line — it gets nudged in a random direction each instant. Those nudges are Gaussian: each step is drawn from a normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)$. So the particle's path is a Gaussian random walk, and the cloud of many such particles is what we see spreading out.

Here is the key idea that makes generation possible: if every step is a known Gaussian, and we could figure out the distribution of each step, then we could run the whole walk backward — start from the fully spread-out noise and retrace the steps until the particles gather back into their original shape. A diffusion model is exactly that: the spreading is the fixed forward process, and a neural network learns to reverse it. You can watch this happen on real data in the panels below.

1 · Forward diffusion q

The data $x_0$ — points forming a dinosaur — is standardized to zero mean and unit variance. That standardization is what makes the fully-noised end state exactly $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, the distribution we later sample from. Forward diffusion dissolves this shape into noise; the trained model learns to rebuild it.

Each step adds Gaussian noise scaled by a small variance $\beta_t$:

$$q(x_t \mid x_{t-1}) = \mathcal{N}\!\big(x_t;\ \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1},\ \beta_t I\big)$$

Chaining the steps has a closed form, so we can jump straight to any timestep $t$ without simulating the ones before it. With $\alpha_t = 1-\beta_t$ and $\bar\alpha_t = \prod_{s\le t}\alpha_s$:

$$x_t = \sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0, I)$$

As $t$ grows, $\bar\alpha_t\to 0$: the data term fades and the noise term takes over, so $x_T\approx\mathcal{N}(0,I)$. Here $T=400$ and $\bar\alpha_t$ follows a cosine schedule (Nichol & Dhariwal), which holds onto the signal longer than a linear ramp before fading to pure noise — small steps, so the shape erodes smoothly.

2 · Training the denoiser

The reverse step is also approximately Gaussian. Rather than predict its mean directly, the network predicts the noise $\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)$ that was added — a simpler, better-behaved target. Training minimizes the mean-squared error between the true and predicted noise:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\,t,\,\varepsilon}\Big[\,\big\lVert\, \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta\big(\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0 + \sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\varepsilon,\ t\big)\,\big\rVert^2\,\Big]$$

One training step: draw a data point $x_0$, a random timestep $t\sim\mathrm{Uniform}\{1,\dots,T\}$, and noise $\varepsilon$; build the noisy $x_t$ with the closed form above; ask the network for $\varepsilon_\theta$; take a gradient step on the MSE. Since any $t$ is reachable directly, no step-by-step simulation is needed during training.

3 · Reverse sampling x_T → … → x₀

To generate, start from noise $x_T\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)$ and undo one step at a time. Substituting the predicted noise into the reverse posterior gives its mean:

$$\mu_\theta(x_t, t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\alpha_t}}\Big(x_t - \frac{\beta_t}{\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}}\,\varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t)\Big)$$

and each ancestral step re-injects a little fresh noise — this is what makes sampling stochastic:

$$x_{t-1} = \mu_\theta(x_t, t) + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,z,\qquad z\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)\ \ (t>0),\qquad x_0=\mu_\theta(x_1, 1)$$

To keep sampling stable, each step also clamps the implied $x_0$ estimate back into the data range (clip_denoised) — standard practice that stops an imperfect model from diverging to infinity.

Where this goes next

The same recipe scales from 2-D dots to images: swap the small MLP for a U-Net, the dino for millions of photos, add text conditioning, and you get systems like Stable Diffusion. Common refinements include the cosine noise schedule, learned variances, faster samplers such as DDIM, and classifier-free guidance.
