The Expectation Behind the MSE

A footnote to Intuitions about DDPM — why a uniform average estimates a weighted expectation

Where did the weights go?

A diffusion model is trained on a single objective[1]. Given a noised input $x_t$ and its timestep $t$, the network $\varepsilon_\theta$ predicts the noise that produced $x_t$, and the training signal is the squared error against the noise actually drawn:

$$\big\lVert\, \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \,\big\rVert^2$$

In practice this is evaluated one sample at a time and averaged over a minibatch, every term receiving the same factor $\tfrac{1}{N}$. But the quantity being minimized is an expectation, and an expectation is a weighted average. The weights have to be somewhere.

The discrepancy is only apparent. Let $f$ be a function of a random variable $\varepsilon$. What a training loop actually computes stands in this relation to the quantity it estimates:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon}\big[f(\varepsilon)\big] \;\approx\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} f(\varepsilon_n), \qquad \varepsilon_n \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1)$$

The left side weights values of $f$ unequally, by the density. The right side gives every term the identical factor $\tfrac{1}{N}$. No factor multiplies $f$ anywhere on the right — and yet the two agree. The weights are not discarded by the average. They are carried by how often each value is drawn.

To see it, forget the model. A weighted average admits two constructions. In the first, each value is multiplied by its weight:

$$0.8\,A + 0.2\,B$$

In the second, each value is repeated in proportion to its weight, and the result is averaged uniformly:

$$\frac{A+A+A+A+A+A+A+A+B+B}{10} \;=\; \frac{8A + 2B}{10} \;=\; 0.8\,A + 0.2\,B$$

Both give the same number. In the second construction every term carries the identical factor $\tfrac{1}{10}$, yet the result is weighted — because $A$ occurs eight times and $B$ twice. The weighting is produced by multiplicity rather than by coefficients.

Estimating an expectation by a sample mean is an instance of that second construction — the Monte Carlo estimate. For $\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$ the expectation weights each value by its density:

$$\mathbb{E}_{\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)}\big[f(\varepsilon)\big] \;=\; \int \mathcal{N}(\varepsilon;0,1)\, f(\varepsilon)\, d\varepsilon$$

The integral ranges over infinitely many values of $\varepsilon$ and cannot be evaluated term by term. Drawing a finite sample and averaging replaces the weight that would have multiplied a value with the frequency at which that value is drawn. Values near $0$ come up constantly; values near $5$ almost never. The density decides how often each region contributes, so the weighting is realized by the sampling itself. The draws pile up where the density is large. Averaging a function over them therefore reproduces the density weighting, though no weight ever appears in the average.

Back to the diffusion loss

The training objective is an instance of the same construction. The squared error is defined for one data point $x_0$, one timestep $t$, and one noise vector $\varepsilon$. But the model must denoise well not for a single triple — it must do so in expectation over all of them:

$$\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{x_0,\,t,\,\varepsilon}\Big[\,\big\lVert\, \varepsilon - \varepsilon_\theta(x_t, t) \,\big\rVert^2\,\Big]$$

Each training step draws $x_0$ from the data, $t$ uniformly from $\{1,\dots,T\}$, and $\varepsilon$ from $\mathcal{N}(0, I)$, then takes a gradient step on the squared error at that one triple. Averaging it over a minibatch is precisely the Monte Carlo estimate of the expectation above. The procedure looks like ordinary mean-squared-error minimization because the weights never appear as coefficients — they are supplied by the three distributions the triple is drawn from.

So the loss is both a mean-squared error and an expectation, and the two descriptions are consistent. A sample mean does not throw away the weights of the expectation it estimates; it represents them in the sampling distribution.
